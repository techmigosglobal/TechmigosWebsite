An MVP is not a small version of every feature. It is the smallest reliable product that can test a meaningful business assumption with real users.

## 1. Name the risky assumption

Write one sentence explaining who has the problem, what they do today and why your product could be better. Choose the assumption that could invalidate the business, not the feature that is easiest to build.

## 2. Map one complete workflow

Describe the trigger, key action, result and follow-up. Include operational work such as approvals, notifications and support. A workflow map reveals hidden scope earlier than a feature list.

## 3. Set release boundaries

Separate must-have, useful-later and explicitly-not-now work. In India, payment methods, language, connectivity and support channels may change the first release shape. Treat those as product inputs, not late surprises.

## 4. Design the smallest testable experience

Prototype the core journey, test it with representative people and record confusion. Accessibility, responsive behavior and error states belong in the first design pass. See our [accessible website planning guide](/blog/accessible-responsive-websites).

## 5. Build with evidence checkpoints

Ship in slices that can be demonstrated. Use QA, analytics and feedback to decide whether the next slice is worth building. A clean backlog is more valuable than a long roadmap no one revisits.

## 6. Define the next decision

Before launch, decide what evidence would make you continue, change direction or stop. Track activation and completion of the core workflow instead of vanity downloads or page views.
